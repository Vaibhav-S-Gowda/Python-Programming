I. OVERALL PURPOSE OF THE SCRIPT
The core purpose of this notebook is to process raw credit risk data and train artificial intelligence classification models to predict whether a borrower will default on a loan. 

In the real world, datasets are messy. They contain blanks, extreme anomalies, and text data that mathematical algorithms simply cannot comprehend. The golden rule of Data Science is "Garbage In, Garbage Out." The first 10 steps of this script exist exclusively to clean, standardize, and balance the data so that when the AI models are trained (in Step 11), they produce highly accurate and unbiased predictions.


II. STEP-BY-STEP BREAKDOWN OF THE PIPELINE

1. Data Loading
What it does: Loads the 'credit_risk_dataset.csv' into a structured Pandas DataFrame.
Why we do it: Data needs to be housed in a programmable digital format before we can manipulate it. It also prints the dimensions (rows and columns) to confirm data integrity.

2. Initial Exploration
What it does: Runs structural commands like `df.info()` and `df.describe()`, and automatically separates numerical columns from categorical (text) columns.
Why we do it: This provides a high-level statistical snapshot of the dataset, establishing what data types (floats, integers, strings) we are working with before attempting math operations.

3. Handling Missing Values
What it does: Automatically locates empty cells (Nulls/NaNs) and fills them using Median Imputation.
Why we do it: Most Machine Learning algorithms will outright crash if they encounter a blank cell. We use the 'median' rather than the 'average' because financial data like income is often skewed by billionaires—the median is significantly robust against extremes.

4. Handling Duplicates
What it does: Detects and removes perfectly identical rows.
Why we do it: If duplicate entries exist, an AI model will inadvertently assign higher mathematical "weight" to those specific users, leading to a biased, overfitted model.

5. Outlier Detection & Treatment
What it does: Visualizes outliers via Boxplots, sweeps out logically impossible variables (e.g. age > 100), and caps extreme financial values using the Interquartile Range (IQR) method.
Why we do it: Anomaly data points severely drag the algorithm's predictions toward the extreme. Removing or capping these values prevents models from failing standard cases.

6. Encoding Categorical Variables
What it does: Translates language into numbers. It uses "Label Encoding" for ranked data (Grade A, B, C becomes 1, 2, 3) and "One-Hot Encoding" for unranked data (Loan Intent MEDICAL vs PERSONAL becomes independent binary 0 or 1 columns).
Why we do it: Machine learning classifiers are mathematical equations. They cannot read English alphabets; they require strict numerical matrices. 

7. Feature Scaling
What it does: Applies StandardScaler to squash continuous numerical values (Age, Income, Loan Amount) down to a shared scale (mean 0, standard deviation 1).
Why we do it: A person's age might be 35, while their income is $80,000. Algorithms often mistakenly misinterpret larger numbers as being "more important." Scaling places all features on a level playing field.

8. Feature Selection
What it does: Evaluates the correlation (linear relationship) between variables and prints out a heatmap.
Why we do it: If two data columns are 99% correlated, keeping both of them creates excessive noise (multicollinearity) and slows down the algorithm's calculation. We identify redundancies to streamline the model.

9. Train-Test Split
What it does: Slices the finished dataset cleanly in two. 80% is the "Training" group, and 20% is isolated as the "Testing" group.
Why we do it: If an AI model is tested on the exact same data it learned from, it is just memorizing the answers. Hiding 20% of the data ensures we can test if the model can accurately predict new, unseen cases.

10. Class Imbalance Handling (SMOTE)
What it does: Deploys Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) natively onto the 80% Training group to artificially balance the amount of Defaults vs Non-Defaults.
Why we do it: Loan defaults are incredibly rare (often 1% to 15% of a dataset). Left untouched, a lazy model could reach 85% accuracy just by blindly guessing "No Default" on everything! SMOTE forces the algorithm to rigorously learn the hidden patterns of defaulting.

11. Model Training
What it does: Instantiates two classical AI Classifiers: Logistic Regression and Naive Bayes, feeding them the perfected SMOTE-balanced training data.
Why we do it: This is the actual AI learning phase. The algorithms adjust their internal weightings based on the cleaned historical variables to map out how they link to a loan default.

12. Performance Evaluation Matrix (Step 3)
What it does: Feeds the hidden 20% Testing Set into the newly trained models to see how they perform. Calculates Precision, Recall, Accuracy, F1 Score, and AUC-ROC, presenting the findings in a side-by-side grouped bar chart and an isolated Confusion Matrix heatmap.
Why we do it: Accuracy alone is a terrible tracker for risk analysis. This precise graphical breakdown proves definitively whether the models are successfully pinpointing loan defaults while effectively avoiding false alarms.
